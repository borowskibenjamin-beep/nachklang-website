Small estate limits, state by state

Many estates can skip full probate using a simplified small estate affidavit – if the total value is under your state's limit. Here's the limit and waiting period for all 50 states plus DC.

Reading time: approx. 7 minutes · Last updated: July 2026

General information, not legal advice, and not guaranteed to be accurate or current. We researched every figure below against state statutes and probate court sources as of July 2026, but probate law changes, thresholds are sometimes tied to inflation indexes, and county practice can vary within a state. Nachklang is not a law firm. Before relying on any figure here, verify it with your state's probate court or a licensed attorney.
Section 1

How to read this table

1

The dollar limit

This is the maximum total value the estate (usually personal property only – see the FAQ below) can be worth for you to use the small estate process instead of full probate. Above this limit, most states require full probate through the court, with an executor or administrator obtaining Letters Testamentary or Letters of Administration.

2

The waiting period

Most states require a minimum number of days to pass after the death before you can use the small estate affidavit – this gives creditors and other potential claimants a chance to come forward. "None" means the state doesn't set a fixed waiting period for this specific procedure.

3

"No fixed cap*" states

Five states (Alabama, Alaska, Georgia, New Hampshire, New Jersey) don't use a single fixed dollar figure – instead they use a formula tied to other allowances, a court petition process without a stated ceiling, or a threshold that depends on your relationship to the deceased. These aren't loopholes; they just work differently. Check with your state's probate court for how it applies to your estate.

Above the limit, or no will at all? See our guide on Letters Testamentary & Letters of Administration for how full probate generally works.

Frequently asked questions

Common questions about small estate limits

What is a small estate affidavit?

A small estate affidavit is a sworn document that lets an heir collect a deceased person's assets directly from banks and other institutions, without going through full probate court – as long as the total estate value is under that state's dollar limit and, usually, after a short waiting period.

Why does the dollar limit vary so much between states?

Each state sets its own probate law independently, so the limits (and even the mechanism – affidavit vs. petition vs. summary administration) were set by different legislatures at different times. Rhode Island's $15,000 and Wyoming's $400,000 are both real, current figures for their respective states.

Does the small estate value include the house?

Usually not entirely. Most states exclude real estate, or count only personal property (bank accounts, vehicles, personal belongings) toward the small estate limit, and separately exclude assets that already pass outside probate, like jointly-owned property or accounts with a named beneficiary. The exact rule differs by state, so check your state's specific statute.
